---
title: "Via Francigena"
summary: "The long pilgrim road between Canterbury and Rome, extended south to Santa Maria di Leuca in Apulia, follows the journey recorded by Archbishop Sigeric around the year 990."
status: reviewed
---

In 990 Sigeric, Archbishop of Canterbury, travelled to Rome to receive his pallium and wrote down the 79 stops on his way back. That list gives the historic skeleton of the Via Francigena, which crosses England, France, Switzerland and Italy and joins the Alps at the Great St Bernard Pass.

From Canterbury the route crosses the Channel to Calais and runs south through the battlefields and plains of northern France, Reims and the vineyards of Champagne, then along the Jura to Lausanne and Lake Geneva. The pass at almost 2,500 m is the high point, with the hospice of the Great St Bernard on top. In Italy the road descends the Aosta valley, crosses the Po plain and climbs over the Apennines at the Cisa Pass. Tuscany is the best-known part: Lucca, San Gimignano, Siena and the clay hills of the Val d'Orcia, before Lake Bolsena and the old Roman roads into Rome.

The Canterbury to Rome section is about 2,000 km. The Italian part is the best developed, with waymarking, guidebooks and a dense chain of parish hostels, religious houses and small hotels. In France facilities are thinner and stages longer, and walkers often rely on gîtes and campsites.

The Council of Europe certified it as a Cultural Route in 1994. In 2019 the European Association of the Via Francigena extended the route from Rome south to Santa Maria di Leuca, where Italian pilgrims traditionally embarked for the Holy Land.

Walkers carry a pilgrim credential and collect stamps along the way. Those who walk at least the last 100 km to Rome can ask for the Testimonium, the certificate of the pilgrimage. Spring and early autumn are the usual seasons; the Po plain is hot in summer, and the Alpine pass is under snow from autumn to early summer.
