The Continental Divide National Scenic Trail was established by Congress on 10 November 1978 and follows the Rocky Mountains through New Mexico, Colorado, Wyoming, Idaho and Montana. Its southern terminus is the Crazy Cook Monument on the Mexican border in New Mexico, and its northern end lies at Waterton Lake in Glacier National Park, on the border with Canada.

Between the two borders the trail keeps close to the Continental Divide, the watershed between rivers draining to the Pacific and those draining to the Atlantic and the Gulf of Mexico. It passes from desert country in the south to high alpine terrain in Colorado and the northern Rockies. In New Mexico water sources can be a long day apart. Colorado keeps the trail above 3,500 m for days at a time, and the CDT reaches its highest point there on Grays Peak, 4,352 m. Wyoming brings the Wind River Range, Yellowstone and the open sagebrush of the Great Divide Basin; Montana ends with the Bob Marshall Wilderness and Glacier.

The CDT is the least developed of the three long American trails, together with the Appalachian and Pacific Crest. Markers are sparse in places, and many sections have official or popular alternates, so no two thru-hikes follow quite the same line. Hikers carry maps or a GPS track and expect road walks where the trail is still unfinished.

A northbound thru-hike usually starts in April or May and takes five to six months. Snow in the San Juan Mountains of southern Colorado is the main obstacle in early summer, and the last weeks in Montana are a race against autumn snow. Glacier and Yellowstone need backcountry permits for overnight stays.

The Continental Divide Trail Coalition, a national non-profit that builds a community of volunteers for the trail, gives its length as 3,100 miles and says that about 160 miles are still incomplete. The USDA Forest Service is the managing agency, working with the coalition and the Continental Divide Trail Society.
