---
title: "Camino Francés"
summary: "The most popular of the roads to Santiago crosses the Pyrenees at Roncesvalles and runs west across northern Spain through Pamplona, Burgos and León to the tomb of St James."
status: reviewed
---

Pilgrims have walked this line since the Middle Ages, and the towns along it still carry the marks of that traffic: hospitals, bridges, monasteries and churches built for travellers. Many walkers start at Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port, take the first day over the Pyrenees to Roncesvalles and then follow the waymarked yellow arrows west across northern Spain.

The route passes through Pamplona, the vineyards of La Rioja around Logroño, and the cathedral cities of Burgos and León. Between them lies the Meseta, a high plateau of wheat fields and small adobe villages, hot in summer and loved or disliked in equal measure. After Astorga the path climbs to the Cruz de Ferro, where pilgrims leave a stone carried from home, then descends to Ponferrada and climbs again to O Cebreiro, the gateway to the green hills of Galicia.

Albergues, pilgrim hostels with bunk beds and shared kitchens, stand every few kilometres, along with private hostels and small hotels. Walkers carry a credential, the pilgrim passport, and collect stamps along the way. To receive the Compostela in Santiago you need to walk at least the last 100 km, with two stamps a day on that stretch, which is why Sarria, just over 100 km out, is the busiest starting point and the last week is crowded.

The Camino Francés is part of the UNESCO listing for the Route of Santiago de Compostela. From the Pyrenees it is about 790 km to Santiago, usually split into some 32 or 33 stages, and spring or September and October are the best times to walk. July and August are hot on the Meseta and beds run short; winter is quiet, but some albergues close.

Many pilgrims carry on for three or four days beyond Santiago to Finisterre or Muxía on the Atlantic coast.
