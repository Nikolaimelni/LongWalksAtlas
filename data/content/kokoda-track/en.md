The Kokoda Track crosses the Owen Stanley Range, the mountain spine of eastern New Guinea, between the foothills near Port Moresby and the village of Kokoda on the northern side. In 1942 it was the scene of a long campaign in which Australian and Papua New Guinean troops first held and then drove back the Japanese advance on Port Moresby, and many people still walk it as an act of remembrance.

The path climbs and drops steeply through rainforest and across rivers, linking villages whose people live along the track and whose land it crosses. It passes the battle sites and memorials of Imita Ridge, Brigade Hill, Templeton's Crossing and Isurava, where four granite pillars stand above the valley. The highest ground is near Mount Bellamy, at about 2,190 m, and nights there are cold despite the latitude.

Most treks take eight to ten days. The southern end at Owers' Corner is a drive from Port Moresby; Kokoda is usually reached or left by a short flight. Trekkers sleep at village campsites and guesthouses, and almost everyone walks with local porters and guides, often from the villages along the track, who carry packs and know the route. Mud, roots and slippery log crossings make the going slow, and the climbs are relentless: the track rarely runs level for long.

The Kokoda Track Authority, a Papua New Guinea special purpose authority, manages the 96 km route for trekkers. It requires a trek permit for everyone, whether walking independently or with a tour, licenses the commercial operators along the corridor, and warns that conditions are hot and humid all year round.

The drier months from April to November are the main season, and the weeks around Anzac Day on 25 April are the busiest. Heavy rain in the wet season swells the rivers and turns the track to mud.
