---
title: "West Highland Way"
summary: "Scotland's best-known long-distance footpath links the edge of Glasgow with Fort William, passing Loch Lomond, Rannoch Moor and Glen Coe on the way into the Highlands."
status: reviewed
---

Opened in 1980, the West Highland Way was Scotland's first official long-distance route. It begins in the commuter town of Milngavie and gradually trades suburbs for woodland, then crosses Conic Hill for a view down Loch Lomond.

The path follows the eastern shore of the loch for two days. The northern half is rough and slow, on rocky paths through oak woods past Inversnaid. North of Tyndrum it follows an old military road across the open peat and rock of Rannoch Moor before dropping to Kingshouse at the head of Glen Coe. The last stretch uses the Devil's Staircase climb to Kinlochleven, then the Lairig Mòr glen and Glen Nevis, ending in Fort William at the foot of Ben Nevis.

Most walkers take six or seven days and walk south to north, which keeps the best scenery for the end. Accommodation ranges from hotels and inns to bunkhouses and campsites, and several companies carry bags between overnight stops. Trains run from Glasgow to Milngavie and from Fort William back to Glasgow.

Wild camping is allowed in much of Scotland, but along the east side of Loch Lomond camping is restricted from March to September and needs a permit in the managed zones. May is the busiest month. The path is open all year, though winter days are short and the moor can be snowbound.

Midges are a nuisance from June to September, especially on still evenings, so repellent and a head net are worth carrying. The weather changes fast at any time of year. Many walkers finish with a climb of Ben Nevis, the highest mountain in Britain, the day after reaching Fort William.
