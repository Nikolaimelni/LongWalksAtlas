---
title: "Pennine Way"
summary: "England's first National Trail runs along the Pennine hills from Edale in the Peak District to Kirk Yetholm just across the Scottish border."
status: reviewed
---

The Pennine Way was Britain's first National Trail. The rambler and journalist Tom Stephenson proposed it in 1935, three years after the mass trespass on Kinder Scout that helped open the moors to walkers, and it opened in 1965. It runs along the backbone of northern England from the Peak District to the Scottish border.

The route crosses gritstone moorland, limestone dales and the high fells of the Yorkshire Dales and North Pennines, then follows Hadrian's Wall before climbing into the Cheviot Hills. The first day climbs onto the Kinder plateau. Further north come Malham Cove, Pen-y-ghent, the waterfalls of High Force and Cauldron Snout on the Tees, the great valley of High Cup Nick and Cross Fell, at 893 m the highest point on the route. The last day crosses the Cheviots from Byrness to Kirk Yetholm, about 40 km with no accommodation on the way, so many walkers split it using mountain refuge huts or a detour off the ridge.

Peat bog and exposed ridges make it a navigation challenge in poor weather. Stone flags now cover the worst of the bog on many sections, but mist on the moors still turns route-finding into the main skill, and walkers carry map and compass or a GPS. The weather can be wintry on the tops at any time of year.

Most walkers take between 16 and 19 days. Accommodation is in villages and small towns along the way, in B&Bs, inns, youth hostels and campsites, though some stages have few options and need booking ahead. Trains reach Edale from Manchester and Sheffield; Kirk Yetholm has buses to Kelso.

Walkers who finish at the Border Hotel in Kirk Yetholm traditionally receive a free half pint, an offer started by the guidebook writer Alfred Wainwright.
